by Hand, under BS 8110

BS 8110-1:1997 · One combined, step-by-step reference from load take-down to detailing

How This Guide Works

Structures are designed top-down but built bottom-up. Load starts at the roof, works its way through slabs, into beams, down through columns, and finally into the foundations. This guide follows that exact order — read it in sequence the first time, then use it as a lookup reference afterward.

New to manual design? Read Part 0 (Fundamentals) first — it explains the shared vocabulary (characteristic vs design load, partial safety factors, K and lever-arm method) used identically in every part that follows.

0.1

Materials and Partial Safety Factors

Every calculation in this guide uses limit state design: real (characteristic) loads and material strengths are each factored to a "worst realistic case" before being combined, so the structure has a built-in margin against the things you can't predict exactly.

Material Partial Safety Factors Table 2.2

Material / Effectγm (Post-Amd 3, 2005)γm (Pre-Amd 3, Original)
Concrete in flexure or axial load1.51.5
Reinforcement (all types)1.051.15
Concrete in shear (design vc)1.251.25

Common Material Grades

fcu = characteristic concrete cube strength — typically 25, 30, 35, 40 N/mm² for RC frames fy = characteristic steel yield strength — 460 N/mm² (high yield), 250 N/mm² (mild steel) Ec ≈ 20 to 30 kN/mm² depending on fcu (for deflection/stiffness calcs)

Design strength is characteristic strength ÷ γm. For reinforcement, BS 8110-1:1997 Amendment 3 changed γm from 1.15 to 1.05. This gives:
• Post-Amendment 3 (Current): Design steel stress = 1/1.05 × fy ≈ 0.95fy
• Pre-Amendment 3 (Older/Textbook): Design steel stress = 1/1.15 × fy ≈ 0.87fy (still widely taught in many universities)

Load Types and Combinations

SymbolMeaning
GkCharacteristic dead load — self-weight of the structure and everything permanently attached (finishes, services, fixed partitions)
QkCharacteristic imposed (live) load — people, furniture, movable partitions, stored goods; from BS 6399-1
WkCharacteristic wind load — from BS 6399-2 (or CP3 Ch V historically)

Standard Load Combinations Table 2.1

Dead + Imposed: 1.4Gk + 1.6Qk Dead + Wind: 1.0Gk + 1.4Wk (or 1.4Gk + 1.4Wk, check both) Dead + Imposed + Wind: 1.2Gk + 1.2Qk + 1.2Wk Minimum dead (uplift / stability / pattern): 1.0Gk

This guide's slab, beam, and column sections deal mainly with the gravity case (1.4Gk+1.6Qk) since that governs the great majority of members; wind/lateral combinations are flagged specifically wherever they matter.

Always check the minimum-load case (1.0Gk only, no live load) on adjacent spans too — for continuous members this "unloaded span" case, paired with a fully loaded neighbouring span, often produces the true maximum hogging moment at a support.

0.3

The Universal Flexure Method (K and Lever Arm)

Slabs, beams, and columns (in bending) are all ultimately designed for flexure the same way. Learn this once; it is reused in every part of this guide.

1 — Compute KK = M / (bd²fcu) — a non-dimensional measure of how hard the section is being worked.
2 — Compare to K'K' = 0.156 (singly reinforced limit, no redistribution). If K ≤ K', section works with tension steel alone. If K > K', compression steel is needed too.
3 — Find lever arm zz = d[0.5 + √(0.25 − K/0.9)], capped at 0.95d.
4 — Solve for steelAs = M / (0.95fyz) — or M / (0.87fyz) for pre-amendment.
5 — Check limitsCompare As against the minimum and maximum steel percentages for that member type.

For slabs, b = 1000mm (design a 1-metre-wide strip). For beams, b = actual beam width (or effective flange width for T/L sections). For columns, the same stress-block logic underlies the design charts in Part 3, just combined with axial load N.

0.4

The Overall Load Path

Roof / Floor Finishes → SLAB → BEAM (if present) → COLUMN / WALL → FOUNDATION → Ground

Every load starts as a pressure (kN/m²) on a slab. The slab converts that into a line load (kN/m) on the beams or a point reaction on columns (flat slabs). Beams convert their loads into point reactions on columns. Columns accumulate every floor above them into a single growing axial force that ends up at the foundation.

Design sequence for a real building: (1) sketch the load path floor by floor, (2) design the roof slab, (3) design the beams the roof slab lands on, (4) repeat downward floor by floor, (5) accumulate column loads all the way to foundation level, (6) design the foundations last.

STEP S1

Identify the Slab Type

Slab analysis branches early. Identify the type first; everything downstream (coefficients, formulas, detailing) follows from this choice.

TYPE 1 — Cl. 3.5.2

One-Way Spanning Solid Slab

Supported on two opposite sides only, or ly/lx > 2.0 on four sides.

TYPE 2 — Cl. 3.5.3.3

Two-Way Spanning Slab, Simply Supported (Corners Free to Lift)

Rectangular panel, ly/lx ≤ 2.0, supported on four sides with no corner restraint.

TYPE 3 — Cl. 3.5.3.4 / 3.5.3.5

Two-Way Spanning Slab, Restrained (Corners Held Down)

Cast monolithically with edge beams/adjacent panels so corners are restrained — torsion reinforcement provided at corners. The most common floor slab type in framed buildings.

TYPE 4 — Cl. 3.7

Flat Slab (Supported Directly on Columns)

No beams — slab bears directly on columns, with or without drop panels or column heads. Punching shear is a governing case.

TYPE 5 — Cl. 3.6

Ribbed / Waffle Slab

Closely spaced ribs (one-way) or ribs in both directions (waffle) with a thin top flange — reduces self-weight on longer spans.

TYPE 6 — Cl. 3.4.6

Cantilever Slab

Projects beyond its support with a free edge — balconies, canopies. Deflection and top-steel anchorage govern.

Decision shortcut: no beams → flat slab. Beams on all 4 sides, ly/lx ≤ 2.0 → two-way. Beams on 2 sides or ly/lx > 2.0 → one-way. Long span, want reduced weight → ribbed/waffle. Free edge, no support → cantilever.

STEP S9

Cantilever Slabs

Mmax (fixed support, hogging, per m) = n × l² / 2 Vmax (at fixed support) = n × l
Basic span/effective-depth ratio = 7 (most conservative of all slab types)

Cantilevers are almost always deflection-critical — check early. Anchor top steel a full anchorage length past the point of contraflexure into the back span.

Never assume a cantilever's self-weight alone "counterbalances" the load — verify the back-span/support has adequate capacity and continuity explicitly.

STEP S11

Detailing (All Slab Types)

  • Simply supported: extend ≥50% of midspan steel to within 0.1l of each support; rest may stop at 0.15l from support.
  • Continuous: extend top steel ≥0.15l into span at interior supports; stop half at 0.25l.
  • Anchorage at simple ends: ≥50% of midspan steel, anchored ≥12φ past face of support.
  • Secondary steel: 0.13% of gross area for one-way slabs (distribution/shrinkage steel).
  • Flat slabs: top steel over column heads per column-strip requirements, integrity steel through columns.
  • Ribbed slabs: check bar spacing within the rib, provide links in ribs as for a beam where shear requires.
  • Cantilevers: never terminate top bars at/before the support — full anchorage into back span.

Common Mistakes

  • Double-counting slab load on both beam and column directly.
  • Using gross wall height instead of clear height between slab soffit and beam/floor below.
  • Forgetting the partition allowance (typically 1.0 kN/m²).
  • Applying live load reduction where it doesn't belong (that's a column-level effect, see Part 3).
  • Ignoring construction-stage loading (wet concrete + formwork on a propped beam below).

STEP B10

Detailing and Curtailment

  • Simply supported: extend ≥50% of bottom steel to within 0.1l of each support end; bend the rest up or stop at 0.15l from the support face with a 12φ anchorage.
  • Continuous — midspan steel: stop 50% at 0.15l from each support face; rest continues to within 0.1l of the far support.
  • Continuous — support steel (hogging): extend ≥0.15l into the span on each side; lap with midspan steel where needed.
  • Cantilever: all top steel must extend a full anchorage length into the back span or supporting member — do not stop at the root face.
  • Link spacing: ≤0.75d throughout; increase near supports where shear is high and reduce toward midspan where shear drops.
  • Minimum 2 top bars + 2 bottom bars throughout regardless of moment (for handling and concrete placement).
  • Bar spacing limits: main bars not closer than hagg+5mm; not more than the cracking spacing from Step B9.

STEP C2

Determine Loading on the Column

Columns accumulate load from every floor above. The load at any level is the sum of reactions from all beams framing in from that floor, plus the self-weight of the column length at that storey, plus the column load already accumulated from the floors above.

Nfloor = Σ(beam reactions framing in at that level) + column self-weight for storey Ntotal = Σ Nfloor for all floors above (cumulative)
Column self-weight: Nsw = 24 × b × h × storey-height (kN, b and h in m)

Live Load Reduction BS 6399-1 Cl. 6.2

For columns supporting more than one floor, the imposed (live) load may be reduced by a percentage that increases with the number of floors, because statistically not all floors are simultaneously fully loaded. Check the table in BS 6399-1 Cl. 6.2 for the applicable reduction factor.

Minimum eccentricity: BS 8110 requires that at minimum, a column is designed for an eccentricity of 0.05h or 20mm (whichever is the lesser) even if the analysis gives a smaller moment — giving a minimum design moment Mmin = N × emin Cl. 3.8.2.4.

Axial Load + Uniaxial Bending

With N and M in one axis, enter the design chart (Part 3c, Fig 3.12–3.21 or tabulated equivalents) with:

Shear in Columns

Columns rarely require shear reinforcement for gravity loads alone — shear usually governs only in unbraced/sway frames under wind or seismic loading. Check using the same method as beams (Step B7).

v = H / (bvd) — where H is the storey shear at that level Compare against vc from Table 3.8 (enhanced by axial compression)

STEP C8

Using the Design Charts

BS 8110 Part 3 provides interaction charts for rectangular and circular sections with symmetric reinforcement. To use them:

Step 1Calculate N/(bh·fcu) and M/(bh²·fcu)
Step 2Select the chart matching d'/h (ratio of cover to compression steel, to total depth)
Step 3Plot the point (N-axis, M-axis) on the chart
Step 4Read across to the nearest steel-percentage curve — round up to a practical bar arrangement
Step 5Verify that the chosen bar arrangement satisfies the minimum and maximum steel limits, and check link requirements

If the point plots below the lowest curve (0.4%), minimum steel governs. If it plots outside the chart boundary, the section is too small — increase b or h and repeat.

STEP C9

Detailing — Columns

  • Minimum 4 bars (rectangular sections), 6 bars (circular) — one bar per corner minimum. Cl. 3.12.5.3
  • Main bar diameter ≥ 12mm; link diameter ≥ 6mm or ¼ × main bar diameter (whichever is greater). Cl. 3.12.7.1
  • Link spacing ≤ 12 × smallest compression bar diameter, ≤ the lesser column dimension, and ≤ 300mm. Cl. 3.12.7.1
  • At laps: reduce link spacing to ≤ 0.6 × normal spacing, with min. 3 sets of links in the lap zone. Cl. 3.12.8.13
  • Where column dimensions change, provide adequate lapping and starter-bar length to maintain load path continuity.
  • At beam-column junctions: provide horizontal links (confinement) within the joint zone per Cl. 3.12.8.1.
  • Kicker: provide a concrete kicker ≥ 75mm above the slab/foundation — steel starter bars must extend adequately past the kicker for the column above.

Part 4

Whole-Building Guidance & Quick Reference

STEP W1

Guidelines for Designing a Full Structure

Phase 1 — Establish the Structural System

  • Choose the structural form: beam-and-slab, flat slab, or combination.
  • Identify bracing: shear walls, cores, or moment frames for lateral stability.
  • Establish grid: typical bay sizes 5–9m for beams, 4–7m for flat slabs.
  • Choose materials: fcu, fy, cover class (Table 3.3/3.4).

Phase 2 — Load Take-Down Floor by Floor

  • Start at the roof: compute Gk and Qk per m², determine n.
  • Design the roof slab (Part 1) → get slab reactions.
  • Transfer slab reactions onto roof-level beams (Part 2).
  • Beam reactions → column at roof level (Part 3, first floor contribution).
  • Repeat for every floor, accumulating column loads downward.
  • Apply BS 6399-1 live-load reduction to column loads at lower levels.

Phase 3 — Member Design Order

  • Slabs (all floors, roof first) → check deflection, detail bars.
  • Secondary beams → primary beams (bottom to top of the load path within each floor).
  • Columns: start from top floor and work down, accumulating N.
  • Foundations: design last when all column loads at base are known.
  • Shear walls / cores: design for wind + gravity, check overturning and sliding.

Phase 4 — Robustness and Tying

  • Provide horizontal ties (peripheral and internal) per Cl. 3.12.3.
  • Provide vertical ties in columns/walls per Cl. 3.12.3.5.
  • Ensure flat slabs have integrity steel through column heads.
  • Check that the structure can survive notional removal of any one element without disproportionate collapse.

Sanity check at every level: sum all column loads at that floor level — it should equal the total factored gravity load on all slabs above (minus the small self-weight of columns themselves). If there's a large discrepancy, trace back to find double-counting or missed load paths.
