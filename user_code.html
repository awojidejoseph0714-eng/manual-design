How This Guide Works

Structures are designed top-down but built bottom-up. Load starts at the roof, works its way through slabs, into beams, down through columns, and finally into the foundations. This guide follows that exact order — read it in sequence the first time, then use it as a lookup reference afterward.

New to manual design? Read Part 0 (Fundamentals) first — it explains the shared vocabulary (characteristic vs design load, partial safety factors, K and lever-arm method) used identically in every part that follows.

Objectives of Structural Design (ULS, SLS & Durability)

Structural design is the systematic engineering process of sizing members and specifying reinforcement to ensure a building safely transfers loads to the ground without excessive deformation, cracking, or deterioration over its design life.

1 — Ultimate Limit State (ULS)

ULS is concerned with safety and structural collapse. The structure must be capable of carrying the design ultimate loads without failure. This includes checks for bending (flexure), shear, axial compression, torsion, and overall sliding/overturning stability.

2 — Serviceability Limit State (SLS)

SLS is concerned with the appearance, comfort, and usability of the structure under service (working) loads. In concrete, the primary checks are:
• Deflection: Slabs and beams must not sag excessively (visual alarm, damage to partitions). Controlled via span-to-depth limits.
• Cracking: Cracks must be limited to prevent water ingress and steel corrosion. Controlled via maximum bar spacing limits.

3 — Durability & Fire Protection Table 3.3 / Table 3.4

Concrete acts as a protective shield for steel. Cover is chosen based on exposure class (mild, moderate, severe) and required fire resistance (typically 1 to 2 hours). Minimum cover ranges from 20mm (mild internal exposure) up to 40mm+ (severe external/coastal exposure).

Materials and Partial Safety Factors

Every calculation in this guide uses limit state design: real (characteristic) loads and material strengths are each factored to a "worst realistic case" before being combined, so the structure has a built-in margin against the things you can't predict exactly.

Common Material Grades

fcu = characteristic concrete cube strength — typically 25, 30, 35, 40 N/mm² for RC frames fy = characteristic steel yield strength — 460 N/mm² (high yield), 250 N/mm² (mild steel) Ec ≈ 20 to 30 kN/mm² depending on fcu (for deflection/stiffness calcs)

Design strength is characteristic strength ÷ γm. For reinforcement, BS 8110-1:1997 Amendment 3 changed γm from 1.15 to 1.05. This gives:
• Post-Amendment 3 (Current): Design steel stress = 1/1.05 × fy ≈ 0.95fy
• Pre-Amendment 3 (Older/Textbook): Design steel stress = 1/1.15 × fy ≈ 0.87fy (still widely taught in many universities)

Load Types and Combinations

SymbolMeaning
GkCharacteristic dead load — self-weight of the structure and everything permanently attached (finishes, services, fixed partitions)
QkCharacteristic imposed (live) load — people, furniture, movable partitions, stored goods; from BS 6399-1
WkCharacteristic wind load — from BS 6399-2 (or CP3 Ch V historically)

Standard Load Combinations Table 2.1

Dead + Imposed: 1.4Gk + 1.6Qk Dead + Wind: 1.0Gk + 1.4Wk (or 1.4Gk + 1.4Wk, check both) Dead + Imposed + Wind: 1.2Gk + 1.2Qk + 1.2Wk Minimum dead (uplift / stability / pattern): 1.0Gk

This guide's slab, beam, and column sections deal mainly with the gravity case (1.4Gk+1.6Qk) since that governs the great majority of members; wind/lateral combinations are flagged specifically wherever they matter.

Always check the minimum-load case (1.0Gk only, no live load) on adjacent spans too — for continuous members this "unloaded span" case, paired with a fully loaded neighbouring span, often produces the true maximum hogging moment at a support.

The Universal Flexure Method (K and Lever Arm)

Slabs, beams, and columns (in bending) are all ultimately designed for flexure the same way. Learn this once; it is reused in every part of this guide.

1 — Compute KK = M / (bd²fcu) — a non-dimensional measure of how hard the section is being worked.
2 — Compare to K'K' = 0.156 (singly reinforced limit, no redistribution). If K ≤ K', section works with tension steel alone. If K > K', compression steel is needed too.
3 — Find lever arm zz = d[0.5 + √(0.25 − K/0.9)], capped at 0.95d.
4 — Solve for steelAs = M / (0.95fyz) — or M / (0.87fyz) for pre-amendment.
5 — Check limitsCompare As against the minimum and maximum steel percentages for that member type.

For slabs, b = 1000mm (design a 1-metre-wide strip). For beams, b = actual beam width (or effective flange width for T/L sections). For columns, the same stress-block logic underlies the design charts in Part 3, just combined with axial load N.

The Overall Load Path

Roof / Floor Finishes → SLAB → BEAM (if present) → COLUMN / WALL → FOUNDATION → Ground

Every load starts as a pressure (kN/m²) on a slab. The slab converts that into a line load (kN/m) on the beams or a point reaction on columns (flat slabs). Beams convert their loads into point reactions on columns. Columns accumulate every floor above them into a single growing axial force that ends up at the foundation.

Design sequence for a real building: (1) sketch the load path floor by floor, (2) design the roof slab, (3) design the beams the roof slab lands on, (4) repeat downward floor by floor, (5) accumulate column loads all the way to foundation level, (6) design the foundations last.

STEP S1

Identify the Slab Type

Slab analysis branches early. Identify the type first; everything downstream (coefficients, formulas, detailing) follows from this choice.

TYPE 1 — Cl. 3.5.2

One-Way Spanning Solid Slab

Supported on two opposite sides only, or ly/lx > 2.0 on four sides.

TYPE 2 — Cl. 3.5.3.3

Two-Way Spanning Slab, Simply Supported (Corners Free to Lift)

Rectangular panel, ly/lx ≤ 2.0, supported on four sides with no corner restraint.

TYPE 3 — Cl. 3.5.3.4 / 3.5.3.5

Two-Way Spanning Slab, Restrained (Corners Held Down)

Cast monolithically with edge beams/adjacent panels so corners are restrained — torsion reinforcement provided at corners. The most common floor slab type in framed buildings.

TYPE 4 — Cl. 3.7

Flat Slab (Supported Directly on Columns)

No beams — slab bears directly on columns, with or without drop panels or column heads. Punching shear is a governing case.

TYPE 5 — Cl. 3.6

Ribbed / Waffle Slab

Closely spaced ribs (one-way) or ribs in both directions (waffle) with a thin top flange — reduces self-weight on longer spans.

TYPE 6 — Cl. 3.4.6

Cantilever Slab

Projects beyond its support with a free edge — balconies, canopies. Deflection and top-steel anchorage govern.
